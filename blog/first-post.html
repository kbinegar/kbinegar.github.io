Blog — July 2026

DRAFT

As we continue to watch Language Learning Models (LLMs), or AI “agents,” absorb larger and larger quantities of labor expectations, many pre-established technical writing professionals have begun considering the security of writing roles. I saw this myself at the 2026 Write the Docs (WTD) Conference in Portland, OR. Hosted in May, several, if not the majority of, discussions were regarding AI—with some notable figures asking the question: “When is it time to stop writing for humans, and begin writing solely for agents?”

As a relative newcomer to the technical writing field, I must admit this question initially felt jarring. It is not that I cannot understand or see the impact and use of AI, but more so that in my previous roles, the act of creation was seen as one done solely by and for other humans—technical in content nature or not.

Existentially, my arguments towards centering technical writing, communications, and the study of information structuring as philosophies worth building my life around ran on the assumption that this labor existed both because of and for human benefit. As someone who left astrophysics due to a missing “human” component, I know this concept does matter to me.

I quickly reasoned that even in the face of creating documentation solely for agents, the resultant impact is still in favor of improving the human user experience. My belief in participating in communication for the sake of improving human experiences can be maintained in the wake of writing for agents so long as it can be assumed that functionality exists as an imperative because of user demand, user needs, and user experiences.

What functionality looks like is bound to change in any field, and technical writers are actively adapting to create documentation that functionally serves LLMs. An ever shifting audience is an ever shifting form of study. This is what I excel in. learning next.
